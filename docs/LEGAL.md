# Legal notes: publishing a Ribbit-style game on GitHub

*Research summary, not legal advice. Written 2026-09-17. If in doubt, ask a lawyer (in the Netherlands
or Germany, depending on where you live) – but for a non-commercial hobby project the practical risk is
low as long as the rules below are followed.*

## Short answer

You do **not** need to worry about copying the *idea* of the game (a grid of letters with connections,
trace words of 4+ letters, unneeded paths disappear, frogs as score). Game rules and mechanics are not
protected by copyright in the US, the EU or Germany. What **is** protected, and what you must not copy,
is the *expression*: the name **Ribbit**, Puzzmo's artwork, sounds, code, colour scheme / "look and
feel", their daily puzzle content, and their word lists.

This project follows that line: own name (**Quak**), own visuals, own code, own puzzles generated from
an MIT-licensed German vocabulary dataset. Published on GitHub with proper licences and attribution,
non-commercial, for learning German. That is the same category of project as the many "Wordle in
language X" projects that the New York Times explicitly said it has no issue with – *as long as* they do
not use the Wordle name or copy its look.

## What the law says

### Game mechanics are not copyrightable
- **US:** 17 U.S.C. §102(b) excludes "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation,
  concept, principle, or discovery" from copyright. Courts have repeatedly held game mechanics
  unprotectable (e.g. *DaVinci Editrice v. ZiKo Games*, S.D. Tex. 2014 – a clone of *Bang!* did not
  infringe; the Copyright Office's Circular on games says the same). Only the *expressive* parts – art,
  text, code, and in *Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive* (D.N.J. 2012) the very specific visual
  presentation – are protected.
- **Germany / EU:** Same principle. A *Spielidee* (game concept) and *Spielregeln* (rules) are not
  protected by the Urheberrechtsgesetz; only their concrete creative implementation is (graphics, text
  of the rulebook if it is more than a functional description, the software). See e.g. the summaries at
  urheberrecht.de/spiele, ludologie.de and the Spieleautorenzunft paper on Spiele und Urheberrechtsschutz.
- **Netherlands:** identical outcome under the Auteurswet – ideas and game systems are free, the
  concrete form is protected.

### What *is* protected and must not be copied
1. **The name "Ribbit".** Names are trademark territory. Puzzmo (owned by Hearst) may or may not have a
   registration, but even an unregistered mark used in commerce can be enforced, and the NYT's Wordle
   takedowns explicitly cited the *name*. → This app is called **Quak**. Do not put "Ribbit" in the app
   title, store listing, domain or screenshots. Saying "inspired by Ribbit (Puzzmo)" in the README is
   fine (nominative fair use / descriptive reference); branding with it is not. Consider renaming the
   GitHub repository as well (`ribbit_deutsch` → e.g. `quak`) – a repo name is public and searchable.
2. **Look and feel / trade dress.** The NYT DMCA campaign against Wordle clones (March 2024, hundreds of
   GitHub notices) targeted projects that reproduced the name *and* the distinctive green/yellow/grey
   tile layout. Non-English versions (Australian English, Yoruba) were hit *because* they copied the
   look, not because of the mechanics. → Use your own colours, tile shapes, typography, animations and
   frog artwork. Do not screenshot Puzzmo or trace their frog.
3. **Code and assets.** Never copy JavaScript, CSS, SVGs, sounds or fonts from puzzmo.com. Reading the
   public description of the JSON format is fine; downloading their client bundle to reuse is not.
4. **Puzzle content.** Their daily boards and solution lists are editorial content. Do not scrape or
   republish them. This project generates its own boards.
5. **Terms of service.** Using Puzzmo as a *player* while building your own game is fine. Automated
   scraping or reverse engineering of their site would fall under their ToS and is unnecessary here.

### The word list you are using
- `vbvss199/Language-Learning-decks` is **MIT licensed** (code and JSON). MIT allows reuse, modification
  and redistribution, including commercially, as long as the copyright notice is kept.
- The repository's `attributions.md` states the decks were built from **wordfreq** (Apache-2.0 code,
  data **CC BY-SA 4.0**). Because our puzzles are derived from that data, the safe route is to
  (a) keep the attribution to wordfreq / Robyn Speer and to the deck repository, and (b) license the
  generated *puzzle data* under CC BY-SA 4.0 (share-alike) while the *code* stays MIT. This project's
  `LICENSE` and `NOTICE.md` do exactly that.
- The example sentences/translations were AI-generated by the deck author and are covered by the MIT
  licence of that repo.

## Checklist for publishing this repository publicly
- [x] App name is not "Ribbit" and does not use Puzzmo/Hearst names in branding. (Repo name: consider renaming.)
- [x] README says "inspired by Ribbit by Puzzmo" once, descriptively, with a link, and states it is an
      unofficial, non-commercial fan/learning project not affiliated with Puzzmo or Hearst.
- [x] No Puzzmo code, assets, screenshots or puzzles in the repo.
- [x] Own visual design.
- [x] `LICENSE` (MIT for code) + `NOTICE.md` (word-list attribution, CC BY-SA 4.0 for derived data).
- [ ] If you ever monetise it (ads, paid app), re-check trademark exposure and the CC BY-SA obligations.
      Nothing in the mechanics stops you, but the "inspired by" reference and the data licence matter more.
